A Lifelong Dream
Born Between Two Worlds

My obsession began with my father taking me all around the world — and with a copy of Marco Polo's Travels that opened a door I have never been able to close. The more I learned, the more I wanted to see it for myself. The Silk Road feels forgotten to the modern world, yet it is still very much alive — in the cities, the food, the faces, the architecture that lines every mile of the route.

I also deeply admire the past. History holds so many secrets still waiting to be uncovered, and in many ways those who walked these roads before us have far more to teach us than we give them credit for. Standing where a Tang dynasty merchant once stood, or sleeping in a caravanserai that sheltered travellers a thousand years ago — that connection to something larger than oneself is what drives me.

Over the years I've managed to visit three cities on the route. Istanbul stopped my heart — that impossible city straddling two continents, its minarets and Byzantine domes jostling for the same sky. Then came Lanzhou and Xi'an, the great eastern terminus, where terracotta warriors guard an emperor's eternal rest. But the vast middle remains a dream I am actively planning for. This site is my living notebook.

History of the Silk Road

✦
Historical Figures
Empires
Trade Goods
Timeline
🧭
Marco Polo
1254 — 1324 CE

Venetian merchant who traveled to China via the Silk Road and spent 17 years in the court of Kublai Khan. His account, Il Milione, introduced medieval Europe to the wonders of the East — or at least a vivid, contested version of them.

✍️
Ibn Battuta
1304 — 1368 CE

Moroccan scholar and traveler who logged over 75,000 miles across the medieval Islamic world, visiting every major city of the Silk Road over 29 years. His Rihla remains one of history's great travel accounts.

⚔️
Zhang Qian
195 — 114 BCE

Chinese Han dynasty envoy who made two legendary journeys westward into Central Asia, establishing the first official contact with Bactria and Parthia. His reports opened the Silk Road for the Han emperors.

🕌
Xuanzang
602 — 664 CE

Chinese Buddhist monk who made an epic pilgrimage to India, traveling through Central Asia and Afghanistan. His account inspired the classic novel Journey to the West. He brought back 657 Buddhist texts to China.

🌍
Timur (Tamerlane)
1336 — 1405 CE

Conqueror and patron of the arts who made Samarkand the most dazzling city in the Islamic world. His empire briefly unified the Silk Road, and his building campaigns produced the Registan and Bibi-Khanym Mosque.

🎋
Genghis Khan
1162 — 1227 CE

Mongol conqueror who devastated much of the Silk Road but paradoxically made long-distance travel safer under the Pax Mongolica. His empire created the largest contiguous land empire in history.

Han Dynasty
206 BCE — 220 CE · China

Opened the eastern Silk Road under Emperor Wu. Traded silk westward in exchange for horses, glass, and gold. Zhang Qian's missions established the first diplomatic links with Central Asia.

Roman Empire
27 BCE — 476 CE · Mediterranean

Insatiable consumer of Chinese silk, Indian spices, and Central Asian goods. Roman senators complained about gold draining eastward to pay for luxuries. The Silk Road connected Rome to Han China without either empire being aware of the other's true extent.

Parthian Empire
247 BCE — 224 CE · Persia

The great middlemen of the Silk Road, controlling trade routes between Rome and China. They jealously guarded their position, preventing direct contact between the two great empires for centuries.

Abbasid Caliphate
750 — 1258 CE · Baghdad

At its peak, Baghdad was the world's largest city and intellectual center. The Abbasid Caliphate facilitated the translation movement that preserved Greek knowledge and connected the Silk Road's Islamic heartland.

Mongol Empire
1206 — 1368 CE · Eurasia

The Pax Mongolica made long-distance travel safer than at any time before or since. The empire created a unified postal system (yam) across Eurasia and briefly made travel from Europe to China possible for individual merchants.

Timurid Empire
1370 — 1507 CE · Samarkand

Timur and his successors turned Samarkand into the jewel of Central Asia. The Timurid Renaissance produced extraordinary architecture, manuscript illumination, and patronage of mathematics and astronomy.

The Silk Road traded far more than silk — it was the ancient world's global marketplace for goods, ideas, and diseases.

🧵
Silk

China's greatest secret for centuries. Romans paid fortunes for it. Smuggling silkworm eggs was punishable by death.

🌶️
Spices

Pepper, cinnamon, cardamom — worth more than gold. The spice trade drove European exploration and colonialism.

💎
Precious Gems

Lapis lazuli from Badakhshan, rubies from Burma, jade from Khotan — traded along every branch of the route.

🏺
Glassware

Roman and later Venetian glass was prized across Asia. Chinese porcelain flowed westward in exchange.

🐴
Horses

The Han emperors desperately wanted Central Asian "blood-sweating" horses. Silk flowed west; horses flowed east.

📿
Gold & Silver

Coin hoards from across Eurasia demonstrate the reach of Silk Road commerce. Sogdian merchants carried coins across continents.

🌸
Cotton

Originated in India; spread westward along the Silk Road. Medieval Europeans were puzzled, thinking it came from woolly plants.

📖
Ideas & Religion

Buddhism, Islam, Christianity, and Manichaeism all spread along the Silk Road. Paper and printing reached the West via this route.

🌿
Medicinals & Herbs

Opium, rhubarb, camphor — the medieval pharmacy relied on Silk Road trade. Doctors prescribed spices from thousands of miles away.

138 BCE

Zhang Qian's Western Mission

Han Emperor Wu sends envoy Zhang Qian westward seeking allies against the Xiongnu. He returns with knowledge of Bactria and Parthia, opening the Silk Road to imperial commerce.

53 BCE

Battle of Carrhae — Rome Meets Silk

Roman legions defeated by Parthia reportedly encounter Chinese silk battle standards for the first time, sparking Rome's obsession with the mysterious eastern fabric.

105 CE

Paper Invented in China

Cai Lun refines papermaking in the Han court. Paper gradually spreads westward along the Silk Road, reaching the Islamic world by 751 CE, transforming literacy and record-keeping everywhere.

629 CE

Xuanzang's Pilgrimage Begins

The monk defies a Tang dynasty travel ban to journey westward to India, traveling through Dunhuang, Turfan, Samarkand, and Afghanistan. Returns 16 years later with 657 Buddhist scriptures.

751 CE

Battle of Talas

Abbasid forces defeat a Tang Chinese army in Central Asia, halting Chinese expansion westward. Chinese papermakers captured at Talas introduce papermaking to the Islamic world.

1206 CE

Genghis Khan Unites the Mongols

Beginning of the Mongol expansion that would devastate Central Asia but eventually unify trade routes under the Pax Mongolica, allowing Marco Polo and others to travel safely from Europe to China.

1271 CE

Marco Polo Departs Venice

Marco, Niccolò, and Maffeo Polo leave Venice for Kublai Khan's court. Marco will spend 17 years in China before returning with stories that seemed too fantastic to be believed.

1370 CE

Timur Founds His Empire

Timur (Tamerlane) begins his conquest, making Samarkand into the most magnificent city in the Islamic world. His patronage of arts and architecture creates the Timurid Renaissance.

1453 CE

Fall of Constantinople

The Ottoman Turks conquer Constantinople, disrupting established Silk Road routes and accelerating European interest in finding sea routes to Asia — directly inspiring Columbus and da Gama.

Into the Unknown

Desert Expeditions

✦

The great deserts of the Silk Road — the Taklamakan, the Karakum, the Dasht-e Kavir — have swallowed entire caravans and made legends of those who crossed them.

🏜️
Taklamakan Desert · Xinjiang, China

Into the Sea of Death

The Taklamakan — whose name roughly translates to "you go in but you don't come out" — stretches 1,000 miles across. Aurel Stein's 1900–1901 expedition recovered Buddhist manuscripts from its sand-buried ruins. The desert preserved for centuries what the living world forgot.

🌙
Gobi Desert · Mongolia & China

Across the Stone Desert

Unlike the sandy Taklamakan, the Gobi is largely gravel and rock. Sven Hedin's four Central Asian expeditions mapped vast swathes of the Gobi and discovered lost cities. The desert contains some of the world's richest dinosaur fossil beds alongside Silk Road ruins.

⛰️
Pamir Mountains · Tajikistan & Afghanistan

The Roof of the World

The Pamirs — the "Roof of the World" — forced caravans through passes above 15,000 feet. Silk Road merchants faced altitude sickness, blizzards, and bandits. The Wakhan Corridor was once a preferred route; Marco Polo described the wild sheep that bear his name here.

🕌
Dasht-e Kavir · Central Iran

The Great Salt Desert

Iran's vast salt desert sits between Tehran and Mashhad. Ancient caravans skirted its edges through the cities of Rey and Nishapur. The desert's surface crust can collapse under the weight of a camel, swallowing men and beasts whole in the salt mud below.

🐪
Karakum Desert · Turkmenistan

Black Sand, Ancient Cities

The Karakum covers 70% of Turkmenistan. Beneath its sands lie the ruins of Merv, once the largest city in the world and a key Silk Road entrepôt. Today the desert holds a curiosity: the Darvaza gas crater, burning since a Soviet geological accident in 1971.

🌊
The Steppe Route · Kazakhstan

Northern Silk Road

Less famous than the southern routes but equally important: the Steppe Road carried goods across the Kazakh steppe via nomadic intermediaries. This grassland highway connected Eastern Europe to China via the Volga, Ural, and Syr Darya rivers.

Preparation & Logistics

Trip Planning Guide

✦

Best Time to Travel

The ideal window for the full route is April–June or September–October. Summer temperatures in the Taklamakan and Iranian deserts reach 45°C+; winter closes mountain passes. The Central Asian spring (April–May) is brief and glorious — the steppe turns green, apricots bloom in Uzbekistan.

Overland Route Strategy

  • Istanbul → Baghdad: Fly Istanbul to Ankara, overland Turkey → Syria border (if open) or via Jordan. Consider the scenic Anatolia-to-Mesopotamia route.
  • Iran Transit: Most nationalities can obtain Iran e-visa (30 days). Enter via Turkey or Iraq at Bazargan/Mehran borders. Tehran → Mashhad by overnight train.
  • Afghanistan: Currently not safe for independent travel. Consider skipping to Uzbekistan via Tajikistan, or flying Mashhad → Dushanbe → Tashkent.
  • Central Asia: Uzbekistan is the highlight — Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara all easily connected by train. Excellent tourist infrastructure now exists.
  • Xinjiang Entry: Travel in Xinjiang (Kashgar, Dunhuang) requires careful preparation. Register at hotels, expect checkpoints. The scenery is extraordinary.
  • China's Hexi Corridor: Jiayuguan → Dunhuang → Zhangye → Lanzhou → Xi'an by train. One of the world's great rail journeys through the Gobi.

Essential Documents

  • Passport valid for at least 6 months beyond intended departure
  • Iran e-visa or visa on arrival (check current eligibility by nationality)
  • Uzbekistan: visa-free for most nationalities (30–90 days)
  • China tourist visa (L visa) — apply in home country or neighboring country
  • Travel insurance with medical evacuation coverage — non-negotiable
  • Scanned copies of all documents stored in cloud
  • Emergency contacts registered with your embassy in each country

Health Preparation

  • Hepatitis A & B vaccinations (required)
  • Typhoid vaccination recommended
  • Altitude sickness medication (Diamox) for Pamirs and Karakoram
  • Comprehensive first aid kit with prescription antibiotics
  • Water purification tablets / filter bottle
  • Sunscreen SPF 50+ and sun hat — desert UV is brutal

What to Pack

  • Lightweight merino wool base layers (works in cold and heat)
  • Modest clothing for Islamic countries (long sleeves, women bring scarves)
  • Sturdy walking shoes + sandals
  • Offline maps downloaded (Maps.me or OsmAnd)
  • Universal power adapter + backup battery bank
  • Small gift items from your home country (always appreciated)
  • A journal — you will want to write things down
